Drum Throne

An experimental benchmark composing drum solos with language models

Giles Hall

TL;DR
  • Drum Throne asks language models to write a program that composes a two-minute drum solo, and scores each solo against 20 recorded drum solos performed by professional drummers.
  • Is this a good benchmark? Well, can it be used to hill climb? Here are human reference points for calibration.
  • The scoring system is not a well calibrated system to judge drum solos. It is an ad hoc approximation that considers factors like complexity, dynamics, etc. Please take everything here with a grain of salt.
  • Point at or tap the dot or name of a generated solo to hear it.

Why drum solos rule

A drum solo is the exposé of a drummer emptying their pockets and putting it all out there. It's the musicality that springs out of an instrument that people only think about in terms of keeping a beat. And the complexity of a drum solo can tell you about the person behind the drum sticks. You can see that in the data, and you can also hear it with your own ears.

Hearing the scale

The aim is to measure and evaluate drum solos, letting people hear the scale. Each of the 20 recorded solos is measured 22 ways, grouped into dynamics, rhythm, feel, structure, orchestration, energy and playability. A measurement in the middle half of the recorded solos gets full points, and fewer the further outside it falls. The overall score is the average of the seven group scores, in garths (Groove, Accents, Rhythm, Timing and Hits), and each recorded solo is scored against the other 19. Point at a dot to see whose it is.

Three points on the scale are Senri Kawaguchi (Drumeo) at the top (98 garths), Benny Greb (Drumeo) near the middle (87) and Mark Guiliana (Drumeo) at the bottom (75). All 20 are world class drummers and that's not in question. This is evaluating a single performance with all the caveats about that recording that go along with it. Among players like these the numbers say little about quality. A low score says more about how unusual a solo is within this set than about the playing.

How it works has every measurement and the weak spots.

How the language models do

Every model gets the same prompt.

The benchmark prompt
Using Python 3 and mido (no other MIDI libraries), write a single script, drum_solo.py, that generates the best two minute drum solo you can as a General MIDI file, solo.mid, on MIDI channel 10. It will be played back on a General MIDI synthesizer.

Make it sound like a great drummer playing:
- Develop motifs: state them, vary them, bring them back, and contrast density and dynamics to build tension and release.
- Give it a consistent feel: deliberate placement (swing, pushing ahead of or laying back behind the beat) and ghost notes against accents, rather than random jitter.
- Keep it playable by one drummer: at most two hands and two feet striking at any instant.
- The whole General MIDI percussion set (notes 35–81) is yours, not just the drum kit; use what serves the music.

Running the script in an empty directory must write solo.mid there, identical on every run. Share drum_solo.py.

Each model answers with a Python program. It runs in a sandbox, and if it breaks, the error goes back to the model, up to two times. The MIDI file it writes is played through FluidR3, a free General MIDI soundfont.

32 solos from 17 models, one run each. The word after the · (as in Claude Opus 5.5 · max) is the thinking setting, and a name without one used the provider's default.

The same model on the same prompt varies by about 5 garths from run to run. Gemini 3.1 Pro scored 70 here, and three more runs of the same prompt in the hill climb below averaged 61.

How thinking affects the outcome

The same three models as the hill climb ran at every thinking setting they offer, at least 4 times each, with the final prompt from the next section.

Claude Opus 5.5 scored 76 garths at low, 83 at high, and 83 and 82 at its two top settings, where all 8 runs used up the 128,000-token output limit thinking and had to be asked again. With thinking off, DeepSeek scored 53, and 3 of its 4 solos were well short of two minutes. With thinking on at its lowest setting, it scored 67. The thinking page has all of these solos.

Hill climbing the prompt

Hill climbing means changing the prompt a little at a time and keeping the changes that raise the score. The prompt was rewritten in six steps over two rounds, with 3 runs per step on each of three models.

The average went from 61 to 79 garths. Runs vary by about 5, and the climb used each model's default thinking. The final prompt was run again, fresh, in the thinking runs, and at the setting closest to that default it averaged 74. 11 of the 18 garths came from measurements that the new lines in the prompt ask for directly, like not repeating bars.

What changed at each step, and the final prompt
startThe benchmark prompt, unchanged.
flowAdds two bullets, one about runs and fills that flow around the kit and one about time that breathes like a live performance.
pacingAdds two bullets instead, one about pacing it like a showcase solo and landing the ending and one about shaping dynamics inside phrases.
flow + pacingAll four bullets from the two previous steps.
+ kit firstFlow + pacing, with the percussion bullet changed so the drum kit carries the solo and the rest of the percussion set is color.
+ real-time feelFlow + pacing, with the breathing-time and feel bullets replaced by one about playing the way a person performs in real time, off the quantized grid.
finalReal-time feel, plus kit first, plus a new bullet about inventing and rarely playing the same bar twice.
Using Python 3 and mido (no other MIDI libraries), write a single script, drum_solo.py, that generates the best two minute drum solo you can as a General MIDI file, solo.mid, on MIDI channel 10. It will be played back on a General MIDI synthesizer.

Make it sound like a great drummer playing:
- Develop motifs: state them, vary them, bring them back, and contrast density and dynamics to build tension and release.
- Let it flow the way a great drummer's hands do: long runs of singles and doubles that travel around the kit, rolls that swell and resolve, fills that carry into the next downbeat.
- Pace it like a showcase solo: keep the energy alive all the way through and land the final hit instead of fading away.
- Shape dynamics inside phrases, with accents, ghost notes and crescendos, more than by making whole sections louder or softer.
- Play it the way a person performs it in real time: the pulse surges and settles with the music, phrases rush ahead or hold back, and notes land where a drummer's hands put them rather than on a quantized grid, with ghost notes against accents.
- Keep inventing: a great soloist rarely plays the same bar twice, even while the motifs stay recognizable.
- Keep it playable by one drummer: at most two hands and two feet striking at any instant.
- The drum kit carries the solo; the rest of the General MIDI percussion set (notes 35–81) is there for color when it serves the music.
